Nikole Hannah-Jones's Daughter Asked, "Did You Know?" I'm Asking My White School the Same Question

Nikole Hannah-Jones's daughter asked whether her parents knew what they were choosing. My mother and her sisters, four of them educators, knew. Then their children came home to teach.

By Eli M. Davis

"Did you know my school was bad like this when you decided to put me here?"

Nikole Hannah-Jones's daughter, Najya, asked her that from the front seat of the car near the end of seventh grade. Ms. Hannah-Jones wrote about the moment last month in The New York Times Magazine. She and her husband had placed Najya in a low-income, mostly Black school in Brooklyn because they believed in it. Their daughter bore the cost of that belief, and she told them so.

I read the essay twice. My family answered Najya's question a generation ago, the other way. Ms. Hannah-Jones and her husband chose the Black school. My mother and her sisters chose the white school. That choice cost something, too. Najya's question belongs at the door of those white schools, and those of us now in our 40s and 50s can answer it, because we were the children.

I made a video about her essay, and it gained a lot of traction. The response that stopped me came from my own family, through very deep discussion in our group chat and in voice notes. I don't think our family ever talked about this, like this before. That discussion is why I wrote this piece.

There is a photograph of my grandmother and her daughters from the 1970s, and in it, my grandmother is laughing. Four of her five daughters became educators, and all of them sent their children to white schools. Aunt Emma was the first teacher in our family, and she taught in the Milwaukee Public Schools until she retired. My mother was a school social worker. Aunt Linda taught. Aunt Deb taught, then became a principal. Aunt Mary alone chose another line of work.

They knew what they were doing. They spent their days with Black children in Milwaukee classrooms, and they knew those schools from the teacher's desk. When our turn came, they put us on buses to the white suburbs or paid tuition at white private schools.

My mother and her sisters were born in the 1940s and 1950s, when schools in Milwaukee and across the country were segregated. Segregated classrooms were the only schools they had ever known. They hoped that in those white schools we would receive a better education.

I was one of those children. My cousins and I all went to school in the 1980s and early 1990s, when desegregation was a political moment and Black families carried the racism that came with it. From seventh grade through 12th, I caught a bus at 5:15 in the morning and rode to Oak Creek, a white suburb south of the city. My cousins went to Shorewood, Whitnall, Nicolet and private academies. One of them rode a cab to Whitnall, because the bus stops sat too dark at that hour.

Milwaukee built that road. In January 1976, a federal judge ruled that the city's school board had segregated its schools. That spring, Wisconsin passed Chapter 220, and that fall the state began paying districts to move students between the city and the suburbs. At its peak, in the late 1980s and early 1990s, about 6,000 students of color were riding out of Milwaukee, while about 1,000 white suburban students rode in. The traffic ran mostly one way. My aunts knew which way, and they put us on the bus.

Black educators have been making this calculation for a long time. In 1935, W.E.B. Du Bois wrote that, in theory, "the Negro needs neither segregated schools nor mixed schools. What he needs is Education." My aunts did that math. Send your child to a hood school and you get what hood schools produce. Send your child to a white school and you get what white schools produce. They chose the white school.

The white schools educated us. They also taught us a second lesson, and every one of us learned it through racialized aggression, and not all of it was physical. A white teacher at Oak Creek unzipped every pocket of my book bag, dumped it out and dropped the whole bag in the garbage. To him I was trash, something to be discarded. A family member who started at a white elementary school in 1985 summed up what she learned in one line: "What's white ain't what's right." A cousin who sat as the only Black student in a college lecture hall said you're "constantly always trying to prove that you're just as good as your white peers."

We came home. The white schools taught us what we were worth to them, and that lesson sent us back. Many of us went on to HBCUs, and everything wasn't roses there, but we don't carry those same stories. It's an African hero story: you get sent off to collect, and you come back and give what you learned to the people, so everybody doesn't have to go through what you did.

I rode that bus out of Milwaukee for six years, and then I came back to teach in the Milwaukee Public Schools. At least eight of us went into schools: my brother, my sister, my cousins and me. Among us are teachers, a principal, an assistant principal, a school social worker and a school secretary. My nephew teaches now too. We went through white schools, then came home to teach Black children in the community we come from, because that is where we feel most like ourselves. Aunt Emma was the first teacher in our family. Now all we are is educators.

My family tells this story in more than one voice. One cousin carries scars from his suburban high school, and he still calls the education he got there "second to none." Today he pays for private school because he wants "safe classrooms, accountability, qualified teachers." I respect that. Each of us who became a parent has had to make Ms. Hannah-Jones's choice inside our own house.

The city school and the suburban school teach the same subjects. They teach different lessons about who you are. My mother and her sisters chose the suburban school's credentials for their children over the Black school's belonging. Ms. Hannah-Jones chose the belonging, and her daughter paid for the missing resources. Both choices cost a Black child something. That trade was manufactured, by income and by redlining, and Black mothers keep getting handed it.

Ms. Hannah-Jones put it best on PBS's "Amanpour and Company" this month: "Nothing is natural about these systems of inequality. We choose them. We structure them in. And so what that means is we can structure them out if we choose that."

The women in that photograph spent their lives inside those schools. They knew them well enough to get their own children out, and their children came home to teach. Black children's brilliance sits in every one of those buildings.

Eli M. Davis grew up on Milwaukee's North Side He is a doctoral candidate in teaching and learning at the University of South Carolina.
